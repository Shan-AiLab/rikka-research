Business knowledge inside an enterprise is scattered across human experience, policy documents, business forms, and different systems. Faced with the same order, different departments and systems may use different definitions, states, and criteria for judgment. People bridge these differences through communication. If agents are to participate in the business continuously, they also need an environment representation that everyone can reference, inspect, and update together.

**Cognito Atlas is a cognitive modeling framework I am exploring: it organizes our understanding of reality into a Cognito Atlas that can be shared, computed over, and continuously revised.** In enterprise settings, it first takes the form of a Business Atlas.

The map needs to answer: which objects—its POIs—exist in the business, how they are connected—which points have routes between them—what state they are in—the current traffic conditions—which rules constrain them, and what a change will affect. For example, how does a contract correspond to an order and a project? How does the project generate material requirements? How does a material shortage then affect procurement tasks and delivery?

Building this map follows four key principles:

- **Define the coordinate system before identifying and constructing objects.** The business domain establishes which part of the business is currently in scope. Perspective determines where the observer stands and which responsibility or problem structures the view. Resolution determines how much detail needs to be visible. Together, these choices establish where to begin, which part of the map to construct, and how to draw object boundaries. The same order, for example, represents a customer commitment from a sales perspective, delivery progress from a fulfillment perspective, and material requirements from a procurement perspective. Only after these observational coordinates are explicit can we decide which objects and relationships belong in the current map while preserving a basis for aligning different local maps.
- **Stable object identities and relationships carry continuously changing states.** The same project may have different attributes and presentation strategies across sales, procurement, and production perspectives, but each view should point back to the same Business Object.
- **Choose perspective and resolution according to the problem.** An executive cares about delivery risk, while a procurement specialist cares about material shortages. They need to share the underlying facts while using levels of detail suited to their respective work.
- **A structured skeleton and unstructured content represent reality together.** Objects, relationships, states, and rules support querying and computation. Drawings, explanations, discussions, and grounds for judgment preserve necessary context and remain connected to the relevant objects.

**These principles also point to a key problem in applying knowledge graphs and GraphRAG: preserving relationships also requires preserving the structure in which those relationships hold.** The real world is a multidimensional network spanning business domains, levels, and perspectives, and each relationship has its own conditions of validity. If modeling flattens those differences and retains only nodes and edges, retrieving relevant information will still not be enough to reconstruct the levels, constraints, and reasoning dependencies required by the current problem. Atlas focuses precisely on preserving those structures explicitly and supporting understanding and use across different perspectives and resolutions.

**Map modeling is a selective abstraction of reality, and the process also exposes parts of reality that have not yet been clearly defined.**

I am applying this method to the design of business blueprints, object relationships, and system boundaries. I am also exploring how a Business Atlas can become an Environment Model shared by humans and agents, supporting cross-system understanding, change tracking, and action feedback.
